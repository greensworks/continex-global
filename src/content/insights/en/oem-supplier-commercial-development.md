For an international manufacturer, entering an original equipment manufacturer account requires more than a persuasive product introduction. The customer needs to understand whether the proposed component or system fits a defined application and whether the supplier can support the relationship over time.

Technical qualification is an important part of that assessment. It should not be treated as equivalent to a commercial award. A disciplined account-development model connects engineering progress with purchasing priorities, delivery expectations and the supplier's ability to respond locally.

## Define the application before broadening the pitch

A product catalogue describes what the supplier can make. An application brief explains where that capability may be relevant to a particular customer. The brief should identify the intended use, the need being addressed and the questions that must be answered before evaluation can progress.

Starting with a defined application helps both sides decide whether a deeper discussion is worthwhile. It also creates a common reference for subsequent technical and commercial work. Without that reference, conversations can expand across multiple products without resolving the suitability of any one of them.

The initial objective is therefore a shared evaluation question, not immediate access to every purchasing category.

## Map the account as a decision system

Engineering, procurement, quality and operations may each evaluate a different part of the proposition. Their involvement and authority vary by customer and programme, so the account map should be confirmed rather than assumed.

For each relevant role, record the issue they need resolved and how their input affects the next stage. A technical contact may be able to clarify an application but not approve a supplier relationship. A purchasing contact may be interested in an alternative source while still needing evidence from other functions.

The purpose of account mapping is to make the decision process understandable. It should support coordinated engagement, with the customer's own process setting the boundaries.

## Run technical and commercial work together

A working plan should connect product evaluation with commercial clarification. While technical questions are being assessed, the supplier can establish the customer's expectations concerning support, communication, delivery and the structure of a potential offer.

The specific evidence and approval steps must come from the customer. They should not be inferred from a generic checklist or from experience with another account. Any requested samples, documentation or reviews need a named owner on the supplier side and a clear understanding of their purpose.

Consider an illustrative situation in which a sample performs as intended, but the support arrangement remains unresolved. Technical progress is real; the account is not yet ready for the same level of commercial commitment. Reporting both facts gives management a more useful picture than a single optimistic status label.

## Make local follow-through accountable

Local coordination should help the parties close information gaps, prepare useful meetings and keep agreed actions moving. It must be connected to the manufacturer's technical and commercial decision-makers, so that customer questions reach people able to answer them.

A local representative without a clear route back to those functions can become an additional hand-off. Conversely, a defined response process allows local presence to support continuity: who receives a request, who prepares the answer, who approves it and who confirms closure with the customer.

## Measure progress through evidence

Meeting counts and proposals sent describe activity. An account review should also show what has changed: the application has been confirmed, an evaluation question has been resolved, the customer has identified the next step, or an outstanding commercial issue has been closed.

Useful review questions include:

- Does the customer recognise the same application and evaluation objective?
- Which function owns the next decision?
- What remains unresolved on the technical and commercial sides?
- Can the supplier support the expectations currently being discussed?

A sustainable OEM relationship develops when the product proposition and the operating commitment remain aligned. Account development should make that alignment visible from the first focused discussion through the customer's evaluation process.
