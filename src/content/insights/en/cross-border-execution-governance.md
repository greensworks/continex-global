An international partnership can have a clear strategic rationale and still lack a workable operating model. The parties may agree on the destination while holding different assumptions about who can approve a change, what constitutes sufficient evidence and when an unresolved issue requires senior attention.

Governance should make these questions answerable in everyday work. Its practical purpose is to connect the people carrying out the project with the people authorised to make decisions. This is particularly important when commercial, technical and local teams work across organisational boundaries.

## Distinguish three responsibilities

The **executive sponsor** maintains the strategic commitment and helps resolve issues that exceed the working team's authority. The **decision owner** approves a defined matter within an agreed scope. The **workstream coordinator** maintains actions, dependencies and communication.

One person may hold more than one role, but the distinction should remain explicit. A coordinator who can arrange meetings may not be authorised to accept a revised delivery scope. Equally, a senior sponsor should not need to approve every routine clarification.

For each important workstream, document the decision to be made, the person authorised to make it, the evidence required and the people who must be consulted. This is more useful than a list of names without a clear connection to decisions.

## Build a shared record of the opportunity

Different teams can leave the same meeting with different understandings. A technical discussion may establish feasibility without confirming price or timing. An indicative volume may support planning without becoming an order commitment.

A shared decision record should distinguish confirmed facts, working assumptions, proposals and approved commitments. Every material item needs an owner and a date for review. Where language differences matter, the parties should confirm the intended meaning rather than rely on a fluent but ambiguous summary.

The record does not need to be elaborate. A controlled brief and a consistently maintained action log can provide a common reference, provided everyone knows which version is current and how changes are approved.

## Design meetings around decisions

A useful meeting has a question to resolve. Participants receive the relevant evidence beforehand and understand whether they are being asked to decide, advise or provide missing information.

If a decision cannot be reached, the output should state why. “Further discussion required” leaves responsibility open. “The engineering lead will confirm the revised specification before the commercial owner reviews the proposal” creates a clear dependency.

The appropriate meeting frequency depends on the work. A critical technical issue may need concentrated coordination; an exploratory partnership may need a lighter rhythm. More meetings are not, by themselves, stronger governance.

## Establish an escalation route

Escalation should be a normal part of the operating model. Define the conditions that trigger it: a decision beyond delegated authority, an unresolved dependency affecting the next milestone, or a proposed change to the agreed scope.

An escalation note should explain the issue, available options, implications and the decision requested. It should also identify the person expected to respond. This helps senior management intervene without reconstructing the entire history of the workstream.

Consider an illustrative situation in which a customer requests an additional product adaptation. The technical team assesses feasibility, the commercial team identifies the effect on the proposal and the authorised owner decides whether to proceed. The coordinator tracks the sequence; they do not silently absorb the decision themselves.

## Keep the model usable

Before launching the next phase of a partnership, leadership should be able to answer four questions:

- Who can make each of the next three material decisions?
- Which assumptions have both parties actually confirmed?
- Where will a disagreement be resolved if the working team cannot settle it?
- How will a change to the agreed scope be recorded and communicated?

A partnership becomes easier to manage when authority, evidence and follow-through are connected. The aim is a decision process that remains clear when the work becomes more complex.
